    Sheet · an essay

    Held, Not Fixed

    Written by
    Helen Edgar
    First set as
    A zine, draft one, September 2026
    This text
    Helen’s draft one, transcribed to a sheet, with the citations added
    Read by
    Helen Edgar and Ryan Boren

    On lost things, wonder, and the cabinets we keep for one another. A shop where a broken object is looked at rather than mended, a herbarium sheet where one flattened plant is made to stand for every other, and a cabinet where a unicorn horn was allowed to stay wonderful after it turned out to be a whale.

    On this sheet

      I · The shop

      What Emily brings Link to this section

      Bagpuss, dear Bagpuss
      Old fat furry cat-puss
      Wake up and look at this thing that I bring
      Wake up, be bright
      Be golden and light
      Bagpuss, Oh hear what I sing

      Oliver Postgate, narration for Bagpuss, Smallfilms for the BBC, 1974. The narration calls these some magic words.

      Emily never once asks whether the thing she carries is broken beyond use. She simply brings it forward, and something in the shop turns toward it with attention rather than judgement. What follows is not a repair in the practical sense. It is a kind of restoration that has nothing to do with function and everything to do with meaning, the sort that is felt long before it can be explained.

      This sheet follows that same found object into two other cabinets that hold lost and overlooked things without asking them to be fixed first: the herbarium sheet, and the cabinet of curiosities.

      II · The sheet

      The herbarium sheet Link to this section

      A herbarium sheet holds a plant that has been pressed flat, dried, and pinned in place, yet what it preserves is never quite the living thing. Botanists call the specimen chosen to define a species its type — the one, as Flannery puts it, upon which the description of a species is based.

      Read the type as a single example held up as the standard, and every living plant after it becomes a measurement against that one: more or less faithful, more or less deviant. It is worth noticing how closely that resembles the way a “normal” mind gets defined — one flattened example made the standard, everything else read as a variation, or a deficiency.

      Some of the artists drawn to herbaria have resisted this flattening. Joseph Beuys pressed plants onto paper and pencilled a title beneath them: Ombelico Di Venere, the navel of Venus. The species attached was Cotyledon Umbilicus Veneris, now botanically designated Cotyledon rupestris — the older Latin carried the navel of Venus in it and the newer Latin does not. Beuys kept the name the textbooks dropped.

      Mark Dion goes further in the other direction. He paints herbarium specimens, exhibits photogravure prints of the paintings rather than either the sheets or the paintings themselves, and leaves the space where the species name would go empty. Both refuse to let the label finish the specimen’s story before the looking has even begun.

      III · The cabinet

      The cabinet of curiosities Link to this section

      Long before natural history collections were sorted into their proper drawers, cabinets of curiosities held the real and the mythical side by side, often with no need to settle which was which. Ole Worm kept what he had been sold as a unicorn horn, doubted it enough to go looking for proof, and in 1638 matched it to a narwhal’s skull. Even once he was right, the courts of Europe went on prizing narwhal tusks as unicorn horns for a long while after, because the wonder of the thing had outgrown its label.

      A cabinet like this asks its keeper to hold two things at once, curiosity and classification, without letting the second one win too quickly. That is not so different from what it means to sit with a mind, including one’s own, that keeps refusing to be filed neatly away.

      IV · The pull

      Monotropic flow Link to this section

      None of this needed to be urgent. Bagpuss did not rush toward usefulness, and neither does a cabinet of curiosities, and neither does a mind following the pull of its own attention wherever it leads.

      What gets called a special interest in clinical language is, from the inside, something closer to a small cabinet of curiosities kept and tended by the person themselves rather than assembled by someone else’s eye. Curating your own wonder, choosing what gets kept and what it means, is a quietly radical act when so much of the world still expects you to hand that decision to somebody else.

      Monotropism is Dinah Murray’s theory, developed with Mike Lesser and Wenn Lawson: attention as a resource that pools deeply in a few interests at a time rather than spreading thinly across many. The reading above is what that looks like from inside a life rather than from across a desk.

      Field note. Restoration of meaning, not repair. Attention, not urgency. A cabinet kept by the one who fills it.

      V · An invitation

      Bring something forward Link to this section

      What is the thing you have been keeping without quite knowing why? A shell, a phrase, a scrap of a television programme half remembered from childhood, an interest nobody else in the room seems to share.

      Bring it forward the way Emily does, not to have it fixed, but to have it looked at properly, turned over, wondered about. Queering Earth is a cabinet of exactly this kind, herbarium sheets for the thoughts and objects and stories that do not need straightening out to be worth keeping.

      Consider this an open invitation to add your own sheet to the collection.

      What this sheet does not settle Link to this section

      Wenn Lawson is named above as he names himself now, while the 2005 paper carries the byline Wendy Lawson. The citation below shows the published byline and the prose does not. On a site about naming yourself that is not a detail to settle silently, and it is the one thing on this sheet that wants a decision rather than a check.

      The zine setting is built, except for the paper size, and that exception is structural. The typography is live in both media — the section rules, the small-caps labels, the tape, the specimen box — and each numbered section starts a new page when printed. What is missing is @page { size: A5 }, and it is missing because an @page rule cannot be scoped by a class. Put in the shared stylesheet it would print every sheet on the site at A5. Scoping it needs a style block in this page’s own head, which is a house question rather than a line of CSS, so it is being asked rather than assumed.

      The section labels are set in small caps rather than the zine’s monospace, on Ryan’s call. A component here may mix the two type roles and may never name a family; the two things that hardcode one both use the reader’s own system face for a semantic reason, and a third added for looks would retire the reasoning behind the first two. Small caps also keep the authored capitalisation in the markup, so the Markdown and the finding aid get The shop and not a shouted copy of it.

      The stamp has not been carried across. The zine’s HELD NOT NAMED mark cannot be .qe-stamp: that component means accession, the number is real or there is no stamp, and a second stamp shape asserting a reading instead of a record would make the real ones unfindable.

      The drawing is a first attempt — a pressed stem with a blank label, which is the middle section in one image. It has not been looked at beside the other eleven sheets.

      The grid of drawers is drawn and the question marks are not. A glyph in an SVG is a text element and this site never ships an image of text, so the argument is carried by the drawing instead: one drawer in a broken line, which is already the house mark for provisional, and one that will not shut because the horn is still in it. The zine’s gold tint went too — --qe-marigold is the seam token and marks an actual repair in the register and nothing else.

      Sources Link to this section

      Emily’s verse is quoted from Smallfilms’ own text — Oliver Postgate and Peter Firmin’s site, which prints the opening narration in full. It is six lines, not five. Two circulating versions are both wrong, in different directions: the reference works give Old Fat Furry Catpuss, capitalised throughout and unhyphenated, and run Wake up, be bright, be golden and light together as one line. Postgate hyphenates cat-puss, capitalises only the first word of each line and the Oh, and breaks the middle into two lines. It is spoken narration and not a song, which matters because the songs in the series are somebody else’s work: the folk songs were put together and performed by Sandra Kerr and John Faulkner, who also voiced Madeleine and Gabriel. Emily was played by Emily Firmin, daughter of the series’ co-creator.

      The herbarium material is Maura C. Flannery, The Herbarium as Muse: Plant Specimens as Inspiration, Biology International 53 (2013), 23–34. The paper is in the house library.

      Monotropism is Dinah Murray, Mike Lesser and Wenn Lawson, Attention, monotropism and the diagnostic criteria for autism, Autism 9:2 (2005), 139–156, doi:10.1177/1362361305051398, published under the byline Wendy Lawson. Helen keeps a resource page at Autistic Realms; Fergus Murray keeps monotropism.org.

      Ole Worm’s demonstration is dated 1638, when he matched the horn to a narwhal’s skull. His Museum Wormianum was published posthumously in 1655 by his son.
