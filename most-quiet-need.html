    Every Day’s Most Quiet Need

    I have spent a lot of my life wanting somebody, at very high volume, and saying nothing about it. The word for that is limerence, and the literature I can find on it reaches for excessive and obsessive and immature almost before it has finished describing the thing. I want to put four objects next to that vocabulary. A piece of music that never resolves. A sonata named after a man who refused to play it. A novel in which one man waits fifty-three years and is not, in the end, wrong. And a sonnet everybody thinks they know, which does something in its fifth line that I have never seen anyone give it credit for. The canon is full of people loving exactly the way I do. It is only a disorder when I am the one doing it.

    On this sheet

      The suspension Link to this section

      Start with the sound of it.

      If you cannot listen to it, here is what it does, because a sheet whose argument can only be followed by ear is a sheet that has decided who is allowed to read it. A figure of a few notes turns over in the left hand. The right hand lays a slower line across it. The figure turns over again, and again, and by the second minute you have understood that it is going to keep turning. Twice the piece shifts into something more agitated and then subsides back into the winding. It does not build to an arrival and then arrive. The winding is the whole event. Eighteen minutes of a thing being held and not resolved, which stops feeling like a delay somewhere in the middle and starts feeling like a description.

      There is a word for the held note that makes this happen, and it is not the one I reached for first. A suspension is a note carried over from the chord before, sounding against a chord it no longer belongs to, and it has to fall to resolve. Two things are true about it at once. It is the past refusing to move on. And its resolution goes downward. Hold onto that; the sonnet at the end of this sheet does exactly the same thing, in words, and nobody reads it that way.

      The carriage in the mud Link to this section

      The other piece I keep going back to is the opening of Beethoven’s ninth violin sonata — the one the world calls the Kreutzer, after a violinist who never played a note of it. It begins alone and unaccompanied, gathers a slow ache of an introduction, and then breaks into a Presto that does not let up.

      I did not come to it that way. I came to it through a film — Immortal Beloved, in which Gary Oldman’s Beethoven is asked what music is, and answers by describing this sonata:

      That is a screenwriter’s Beethoven, not Beethoven, and the distinction is worth keeping because the line is doing neatly what the real history does messily. Beethoven wrote a letter in July 1812 to a woman he addressed as the Immortal Beloved. It was never delivered. Nobody has established for certain who she was; the film picks somebody, and the letter does not. What survives is the reaching, with the destination struck out. Rose invented a carriage to stand in a place that is genuinely empty.

      And the wheels-in-the-mud image is the right one for a reason the film does not go into. The agitation is a function of the barrier, not of the beloved. A man who gets there is not making that sound.

      The physics of the barrier Link to this section

      Somebody has already written the mechanism down precisely, and put it in the mouth of the villain.

      Mond is the Resident World Controller for Western Europe and he is not praising longing. He is explaining why his civilisation abolished it. Remove the interval between wanting a thing and having it, take down the barrier, and there is no flood — no feeling, no passion, no madness, and no further need for any of them. He calls what is left stability, and the whole book is the argument that he got exactly what he asked for.

      I take his description and refuse his prescription. The physics is right: the height of the barrier sets the force of the flood, which is why the sonata sounds like that and why the eighteen minutes wind. But a society that engineers away every obstacle so nobody ever floods has not cured longing. It has removed the conditions under which a person could want something they do not have, which is most of what I mean when I say I am alive.

      Say it plainly, because the same move gets made about limerence constantly, wearing a lab coat. The flood gets treated as the pathology. Almost nobody asks what the barrier was, or who built it, or why the current was running that hard to begin with.

      And Mond’s hydraulics are not a novelist’s conceit. Dorothy Tennov, who coined the word limerence, spent years collecting accounts of it, and the barrier is in her findings as a plain condition of the thing:

      Her list of the state’s basic components carries the same fact in three words: intensification through adversity. Mond says the height of the barrier sets the force of the flood; Tennov found it by asking several hundred people. An externally imposed obstacle, such as Romeo and Juliet met in the resistance of family and society — hold that sentence until the end of this sheet, where a woman writes forty-four sonnets she does not show anybody, in a house with a father in it.

      Fifty-three years, seven months, and eleven days Link to this section

      My obsessive windings like stories of archaic and boldly romantic love, and Love in the Time of Cholera is the cathedral of them. Florentino Ariza waits for Fermina Daza for fifty-three years, seven months, and eleven days and nights. He does not stop. He is not cured.

      García Márquez knows precisely what he is describing, and he has the local doctor say it out loud:

      It is a joke and it is also the title of the book. Lovesickness presents as an epidemic disease; the weak pulse, the pallor, the urgent wish to die. The doctor prescribes linden blossom and a change of air, which is the nineteenth century’s go and touch some grass, and the patient declines it:

      I recognise that, and not the martyrdom part. The refusal. There is something inside the state that I did not want to be talked out of, because being talked out of it would have meant the thing I was reaching toward was never worth the reaching, and I knew that it was.

      On the shelf, and in the clinic Link to this section

      Here is the thing this sheet is actually for.

      Everything above is one kind of attention doing one kind of thing. A man holds a single channel open for fifty-three years and will not be redirected. A composer never gets over a letter he did not send and writes the ache into a sonata. A pianist writes eighteen minutes that refuse to arrive. Those are masterpieces. We give them Nobel prizes and concert halls and a shelf each. When people describe what those works are about, the words are great and enduring and the immense ache of this world.

      Now take the identical behaviour — attention that pools around one person, will not redirect, replays every exchange, survives years without reciprocation, refuses the cure — and put it in an Autistic person. The words change completely. Excessive. Obsessive. Immature. Unhealthy. Those four are not my straw men; they are the list Helen Edgar gives of how the literature actually frames limerence when it mentions autism at all.

      Same behaviour. Masterpiece on the shelf, symptom in the clinic. The only variable that moved is who was doing it.

      I do not think the canon is wrong about Florentino. I think the clinic is wrong about me, and I think the canon is the evidence. A culture that has built its highest romantic art out of unreciprocated single-channel devotion does not get to turn round and call that devotion a deficit when a monotropic person arrives holding it. Either the sonata is a symptom too, or my longing is not one.

      The lake Link to this section

      Helen Edgar and Stimpunks put a Lake of Limerence on the Map of Monotropic Experiences. It sits on the same island as the River of Monotropic Flow States, and the difference between those two pieces of water is the whole of what I am trying to say. A river has an outlet. A lake does not. Water gathers in a lake and stays.

      The map calls that a stuck state, as against a flow state, and stuck is not a synonym for bad. It is the same attention that gives me flow, pooled instead of running. Monotropism does not keep a separate mode for people. When something becomes emotionally significant my attention gathers around it and will not be redirected, and it draws no distinction whatever between a research question and somebody who was kind to me once.

      None of which is a stretch laid over Tennov’s word from outside. It is in her definition. Among the basic components she lists in 1979, between the intrusive thinking and the fear of rejection, is this one: inability to react limerently to more than one person at a time. A quarter of a century before monotropism had a name, the woman who named limerence had written single-channel attention into what the thing is. She did not mean anything about autism by it. She was describing what several hundred people told her it was like.

      Here is mine.

      I felt limerence hard in high school. It was overwhelming. I never said anything to the person. I didn’t want my feelings to be their problem.

      I could fall hard for people who were kind to me. The smallest kindness made them bright in my eyes. I never said anything. I tried to be a good friend.

      I was the stereotypical Autistic kid in that I was awkward, afraid, and alone. My first date and first kiss were in my mid twenties. I didn’t think that would ever happen.

      I had no co-regulation at home. My parents wouldn’t let me in their bed when I was terrified at night. I slept in the hallway, on the hardwood floor, with the lights on. No hugging. No cuddling. Ever.

      What the lake was for Link to this section

      A lot of the power of my limerence probably came from that desperate need for kindness and co-regulation.

      That is the part the pathology framing cannot see from where it stands. My limerence was not misfiring desire. It was an accurate instrument pointed at a real absence. I experience it as a longing for connection, belonging, and legibility — which is to say, for precisely the things that were not in that hallway. Anissa Ljanta describes limerence as a coping strategy, a way a nervous system reaches for regulation through imagined closeness. Read like that, the flood is not the disease. The flood is the reading on the gauge.

      There is a second hazard in the lake and it took me decades to name. Several times in my career I had a work-wife or a work-husband, somebody I teamed up with constantly. Package deals. I have come to think about those in terms of mutual limerence and memory-foaming — Attlee Hall’s word for the Autistic habit of taking the shape of whatever you come into contact with, conceding and bending and making yourself agreeable to the point of self-alienation:

      So the danger of the lake is not only that you cannot get out of it. It is that you dissolve in it. You mould to the person you are longing toward, become soft and accommodating and easy to have around, and after a while the longing is hard to tell apart from the disappearing. You are reaching for legibility by making yourself illegible. It does not work, and it cannot work, and that is a structural fact about the method rather than a shortcoming in the person.

      My heart’s all over the place.

      And then the timing is right Link to this section

      Stories of bad timing and could-have-beens. Lives lived longing for what never was.

      And then, one day, the timing is right, and the could-have-been becomes the requited actually.

      Florentino gets his. It takes fifty-three years and a riverboat flying a cholera flag so they will not have to put in anywhere. And what he imagines on the way to it is not the flood at all:

      One love for everyday use. Fifty-three years of the most operatic longing in the language, and the thing he is finally reaching for is domestic. Not the flood. The channel.

      And notice what requital is doing there, because it is not what the clinic would do. Nobody treated Florentino. Nobody redirected his attention, distracted him, prescribed him an interest, or talked him out of it. The stuck state ended because somebody arrived in it. That is co-regulation, not therapy, and the difference is the whole argument: you do not fix a lake by draining it, you give it a river.

      Every day’s most quiet need Link to this section

      It is probably the most quoted love poem in English, which is a good way for a poem to become invisible. Read the first five lines slowly.

      The soul reaches to the end of its own capacity — the depth and breadth and height, three dimensions of it, a measured volume — and it is reaching when feeling out of sight. In the dark. Toward something not there. That is the lake, and the suspension, and a man in a carriage with the wheels stuck. Four lines of pure unreciprocated reaching, at the top of the poem, in the register of the sublime.

      And then the poem does something no one expects a love sonnet to do. It comes down.

      I love thee to the level of every day’s / Most quiet need, by sun and candle-light. Level, in a poem that has just finished measuring height. The flood arriving at grade. It is the same move the suspension makes in music: the held thing finally falls, and falling is how it resolves.

      The standard reading treats that line as the modest beat — the humble domestic interlude before the sonnet climbs back up to saints and God and death, where the real ambition supposedly lives. I think it is the most ambitious line in the poem, and I think the poem knows it. It is the only line that names both the sun and a candle: daylight and night, the whole revolution of one ordinary day. Anyone can love at altitude for an evening. Sustaining it at the level of every day’s most quiet need — every day, at both ends of every day, at the unglamorous scale of what a person actually needs — is the hard thing.

      And read from a bodymind that has never had it, it is not the modest ask. It is the enormous one. I knew what altitude felt like by fifteen. I did not know what the level felt like until I was well into my twenties. The sublime was never the part I could not imagine. The quiet need was.

      Elizabeth Barrett and Robert Browning wrote to each other across a barrier of the kind Mond wanted abolished: her father, her illness, a courtship conducted largely in letters, a marriage made in secret and paid for with her disinheritance. The sonnets were written during that, and she did not show them to him until afterwards. The height of the barrier set the force of the flood. And then they eloped, and had fifteen years in Florence, and the flood went where Huxley’s villain says it goes: down its appointed channels into a calm well-being.

      Which is Mond’s sentence, and I have taken it off him. He meant it as an argument for never damming anything. I mean it as a description of what it is like when the dam finally opens.

      The swell and the dwell Link to this section

      I wrote the other half of this on Dickinson, on Sheet No. 6, and it turns out to have been about this all along:

      Everything on this sheet is a swell that was about a dwell. That is the thing I did not understand for about thirty years. I thought I was having enormous feelings about particular people. What I was doing was longing, at maximum volume, for a condition — connection, belonging, legibility — that I had no experience of, and so could only picture attached to somebody’s face.

      It also explains why the memory-foaming and the limerence ran together in me. If what you want is to be legible, and the only route you have ever found to being kept is to become the shape of the room, then you will pursue legibility by way of self-erasure, indefinitely, and never arrive. The lake has no outlet because you keep filling it with yourself.

      And the last word on that should go to the person who named the state, in the same paragraph in which she named it:

      She is pointing at the dwell. Not as a consolation for people who failed at the swell, but as the thing that was more important all along, said by the one person with the least reason to undersell limerence — she gave it its name and a book.

      The dwell is the outlet. Not because being loved back cures the longing — it does not, and I would not want it to. Because being met as yourself is the single thing that stops the reaching being the only activity available. You can put down the compass. That is not the end of love. That is love at the level of every day’s most quiet need, which is where the sonnet said it was going in its fifth line, before anybody was listening.

      Florentino is asked, on the last page, how long they can keep going up and down the river like this.

      What this sheet does not settle Link to this section

      Left open on purpose

      • Every object on this sheet assumes love ends in two people, and I do not. Browning’s sonnet is addressed to one thee. Florentino wants one love for everyday use. The marriage plot, the elopement, the riverboat: all of it terminates in a dyad. Elizabeth Brake’s word for that assumption is amatonormativity — the belief that a central exclusive romantic pairing is the proper destination of a life. My monotropic attention does not produce one beloved. It produces many, in sequence and at once, and a work-spouse is one of the shapes it takes. Which is a real problem for the vocabulary, and I would rather leave it visible than solve it with a word. Tennov’s own criteria include the inability to react limerently to more than one person at a time, so on a strict reading the wider thing I am describing — wanting to co-regulate with nearly everybody — is not limerence at all, whatever the episodes with single people were. I do not know whether that means her definition is too narrow, or my word is too loose, or the two states are genuinely different and I have been calling both by the louder name. There are so many ways to love and people to love. I fall for everyone. I want to co-regulate with everyone. Monogamy does not make sense to me, though I am well-practised at it at this point. The canon only knows how to hand me one, and that is a limit of the canon rather than of me. This sheet arrives at the dwell and stops there; the question of how many dwells a person is allowed is a different sheet and it has not been written.
      • Tennov is now read first-hand; the monotropism literature is not. Every Tennov passage on this sheet was read in Love and Limerence itself. Dinah Murray, Wenn Lawson and Mike Lesser set out monotropism in 2005, and McDonnell and Milton describe attention clumping in 2014; both reached this sheet through Helen Edgar’s essay, which cites them properly, and neither primary has been opened here.
      • Opening Tennov corrected an attribution this sheet was about to make. A draft said she described the crystallisation that idealises the limerent object, which is true and misleading in the way that matters. Crystallisation is Stendhal’s, from De l’Amour in 1822 — the branch thrown into a salt mine and taken out months later as an object of shimmering beauty — and Tennov says so plainly on her page 30, calling it the term he gave. She is the one who carried it forward, and he is the one it belongs to. It reached us as hers because we had read a summary rather than the book, which is the whole argument for reading the book.
      • The Love in the Time of Cholera passages were found in one copy and paginated from another, and both are named because neither would be honest alone. The wording was read and checked character for character against a scan Ryan holds, which carries no usable pagination and is not an authorised copy. The page numbers come from his Vintage International ebook, whose reader shows a publisher-supplied page map — checked by changing the text size and confirming the numbers do not move, which is the difference between a book’s pagination and a device’s. A number that shifted with a font setting would be worse than none: it would look exact and be unreproducible for every other reader.
      • The Immortal Beloved scene is linked to an upload the rightsholder is not paid for. Our policy asks for a copy whose owner gets something, and for this film we could not find one — no official clip, no licensed excerpt. The two recordings this sheet embeds both come from the rightsholders’ own deliveries, Sony Classical and Warner Classics. The film scene gets a plain link instead, so that following it is the reader’s decision and not a request this page makes on their behalf. It is a relaxation of the rule and it is recorded as one.
      • Whether a stuck state ever simply ends is not something one life can answer. Mine did, once, and this sheet is honest that the ending arrived from outside rather than from anything I did. That is a reason to build the conditions for co-regulation and a poor reason to tell anybody currently in the lake to wait. I do not know what to say to the version of me who is still in the hallway, except that the reaching was not the fault.
