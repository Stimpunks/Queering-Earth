What this is Link to this section

Queering Earth reads the world through a queering lens — art, literature, poetry, politics, people, and history — and asks what else any of it could have been.

Our sibling site, Star Stuff, is built on science: bone that turns pressure into charge, atoms forged in dying stars, a universe that declines to pathologize its own variation. This place is built on the other half of the shelf — the humanities, the myths, the canon, the stories we were handed and told not to question.

There are no hard facts here for everything. That is deliberate. Interpretation is the method, and the room left open is where the reading happens.

Queering is a verb Link to this section

Walker is naming two normativities in particular. Taking the verb further out — to a painting, a canon, a century, a diagnosis, a life — is our extension, not his sentence. The credit for the verb stays with him.

The line under our masthead owes him too. Neuroqueer Heresies is subtitled Notes on the Neurodiversity Paradigm, Autistic Empowerment, and Postnormal Possibilities — one hyphen from our own. Whatever route the phrase took to reach us, he published it first, and a site about queering normativity is not going to be coy about where its masthead came from.

To queer is to adapt Link to this section

Walker gives us the verb. Pamela Demory — writing in adaptation studies, not in neurodiversity — gives us what the verb does to a text. Her argument is that two fields have been describing the same motion under different names for years, and she sets the two words on either side of a slash to say so.

She arrives where Walker is standing, by the other road: If we think of queer as a verb, as a doing rather than a being, then to queer something is to deconstruct it, to demonstrate the instability of all those apparently obvious oppositions — and the three she names are male/female, homosexuality/heterosexuality, and normal/deviant.

That is this site’s method described from the outside. Every sheet here is an adaptation: Woolf read through illness, Rossetti read through solo polyamory, Miranda read as somebody to be wondered with. Demory’s useful insistence is that this is not a lesser thing to do to a text. Adaptation and queerness both, she writes, suffer from the stereotype of being secondary, somehow less authentic — a copy held against an original, a life held against a norm. It is one charge made twice, and it is the charge this site declines.

The sentence adapts itself Link to this section

Five years later Demory quotes that sentence back, in a chapter on Apple TV+’s Dickinson. She does not quote it whole. She cuts it to what the new argument needs, marks every cut, and leaves her own name on what is left.

Read the two together and the second one performs the first. To evolve, to repeat, imitate, parody, make new is gone. But also to turn is gone. To adapt is to queer — the half of the sentence that ran the other way — is gone. What survives is still hers and still true, and it is no longer the same sentence. It is repetition, but repetition without replication, which is Linda Hutcheon’s definition of adaptation, quoted in Demory’s own 2019 introduction three pages after the sentence she would later cut.

An author may do that to her own line. The reason it reads as adaptation and not as misquotation is the ellipses: every cut is marked, and you can see exactly where the scissors went. That is the whole difference, and it is not a small one. A trimmed quotation with the attribution still attached is the characteristic failure of a site like this one — not an invented source, a tightened one. So we mark the cuts, or we do not make them, and when we get it wrong we say so in the register.

The type plays, and the words do not move Link to this section

Letters on this site lean, deviate, and refuse to sit on the line. That is not decoration. A page about normativity that sets itself in a tidy grid is arguing against itself.

It also is not a trade. Every page here has a plain view — the control at the top of this one. It turns off the play, the motion, and the display faces, and leaves the words exactly as they are. One page, two ways to read it, and no second-class copy. Nothing on this site will ever be readable only in the decorated version.

What grows here Link to this section

Seven sheets, so far, mounted and numbered on the plate below.

  • Reading No. 1 The Army of the Upright Virginia Woolf, On Being Ill, 1930 Illness missing from the prime themes, a language that runs dry, and the army you desert by lying down.
  • Essay No. 2 Coming to Terms Ryan Boren, 2022 Pink-tinted eyeglasses in Southern Baptist Texas, and two words that fit after a lifetime of looking.
  • Reading No. 3 The Die Uncast Christina Rossetti, Promises like Pie-crust, 1861 Twenty-four lines refusing to promise anybody anything, read as the charter for solo polyamory that nobody handed us.
  • Reading No. 4 Wyrd, Weird, and the Invention of Normal Helen Edgar, More Realms, July 2026 A word demoted from fate to defect, three sisters at a well, and an idea of normal barely two centuries old.
  • Reading No. 5 Miranda: To Be Wondered With William Shakespeare, The Tempest, 1611 A name coined from the Latin for wonder at, a boy actor in 1611, and the editor who moved her hardest speech into her father’s mouth.
  • Reading No. 6 The Swell and the Dwell Emily Dickinson, Wild nights – Wild nights!, about 1861 Twelve lines asking for one night in harbour, with no indicative verb in them, read as co-regulation and answered in verse.
  • Wall No. 8 The Preferred Flower of Life Monotropa uniflora, Dickinson, and monotropism A plant that will not photosynthesise, the flower Dickinson kept in her herbarium, and one Greek root shared by a 1753 binomial and a theory of Autistic attention.
  • Reading No. 7 A Waste Garden, Flowering at Its Will Violet, pansy, green carnation, and lavender Four flowers worn where they could be seen, each read in the end by the people it was meant to slip past.

The border is four flowers that were once a way of saying it without saying it: violet, pansy, green carnation, lavender. Gathered as a set by Sarah Prager in Four Flowering Plants That Have Been Decidedly Queered, JSTOR Daily, 29 January 2020.
