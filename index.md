## What this is

Queering Earth reads the world through a queering lens — art, literature, poetry, politics, people, and history — and asks what else any of it could have been.

Our sibling site, [Star Stuff](https://starstuff.earth/), is built on science: bone that turns pressure into charge, atoms forged in dying stars, a universe that declines to pathologize its own variation. This place is built on the other half of the shelf — the humanities, the myths, the canon, the stories we were handed and told not to question.

There are no hard facts here for everything. That is deliberate. Interpretation is the method, and the room left open is where the reading happens.

## Queering is a verb

The word we are working with comes from Queer Theory, and [Nick Walker](https://neuroqueer.com/) carried it into neurodiversity. His definition is a parenthesis, and the parenthesis is the whole thing:

Specimen — the verb

> I originally conceived of *neuroqueer* as a verb: neuroqueering as the practice of queering (subverting, defying, disrupting, liberating oneself from) neuronormativity and heteronormativity simultaneously.

Nick Walker, [*Neuroqueer: An Introduction*](https://neuroqueer.com/neuroqueer-an-introduction/). Collected in [*Neuroqueer Heresies*](https://neuroqueer.com/neuroqueer-heresies/) (Autonomous Press, 2021).

Walker is naming two normativities in particular. Taking the verb further out — to a painting, a canon, a century, a diagnosis, a life — is our extension, not his sentence. The credit for the verb stays with him.

The line under our masthead owes him too. *Neuroqueer Heresies* is subtitled *Notes on the Neurodiversity Paradigm, Autistic Empowerment, and Postnormal Possibilities* — one hyphen from our own. Whatever route the phrase took to reach us, he published it first, and a site about queering normativity is not going to be coy about where its masthead came from.

After the cover of Nick Walker’s [*Neuroqueer Heresies*](https://neuroqueer.com/neuroqueer-heresies/), which sets a human brain where a swallowtail’s body would be, and after Ezra Furman’s [*Lilac and Black*](https://genius.com/Ezra-furman-lilac-and-black-lyrics). Ours is a moth, and the Earth is where the brain was.

## To queer is to adapt

Walker gives us the verb. [Pamela Demory](https://ucdavis.academia.edu/PDemory) — writing in adaptation studies, not in neurodiversity — gives us what the verb does to a text. Her argument is that two fields have been describing the same motion under different names for years, and she sets the two words on either side of a slash to say so.

Specimen — the slash

> To adapt is to modify, to evolve, to transform, to repeat, imitate, parody, make new. To queer something is to make it strange or odd, but also to turn or transform it. To queer, then, may be to adapt; to adapt is to queer.

Pamela Demory, [*Queer/Adaptation: An Introduction*](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-030-05306-2_1), page 1. In [*Queer/Adaptation: A Collection of Critical Essays*](https://link.springer.com/book/10.1007/978-3-030-05306-2) (Palgrave Macmillan, 2019).

She arrives where Walker is standing, by the other road: “If we think of queer as a verb, as a doing rather than a being, then to queer something is to deconstruct it, to demonstrate the instability of all those apparently obvious oppositions” — and the three she names are male/female, homosexuality/heterosexuality, and normal/deviant.

That is this site’s method described from the outside. Every sheet here is an adaptation: Woolf read through illness, Rossetti read through solo polyamory, Miranda read as somebody to be wondered *with*. Demory’s useful insistence is that this is not a lesser thing to do to a text. Adaptation and queerness both, she writes, “suffer from the stereotype of being secondary, somehow less authentic” — a copy held against an original, a life held against a norm. It is one charge made twice, and it is the charge this site declines.

## The sentence adapts itself

Five years later Demory quotes that sentence back, in a chapter on Apple TV+’s *Dickinson*. She does not quote it whole. She cuts it to what the new argument needs, marks every cut, and leaves her own name on what is left.

Specimen — the same sentence, adapted

> As argued elsewhere, queer and adaptation are parallel terms, both shaping the ways that we approach or reflect on given texts. In other words, ‘To adapt is to modify … To queer something is to make it strange or odd … or transform it. To queer, then, may be to adapt’ (Demory 2019).

Pamela Demory, [*Queering Emily Dickinson for the Millennial Age*](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-031-50832-5_10). In Blythe Worthy and Paul Sheehan, eds., *Adapting Television and Literature* (Palgrave Macmillan, 2024).

Read the two together and the second one performs the first. *To evolve, to repeat, imitate, parody, make new* is gone. *But also to turn* is gone. *To adapt is to queer* — the half of the sentence that ran the other way — is gone. What survives is still hers and still true, and it is no longer the same sentence. It is “repetition, but repetition without replication”, which is Linda Hutcheon’s definition of adaptation, quoted in Demory’s own 2019 introduction three pages after the sentence she would later cut.

An author may do that to her own line. The reason it reads as adaptation and not as misquotation is the ellipses: every cut is marked, and you can see exactly where the scissors went. That is the whole difference, and it is not a small one. A trimmed quotation with the attribution still attached is the characteristic failure of a site like this one — not an invented source, a tightened one. So we mark the cuts, or we do not make them, and when we get it wrong we say so in [the register](https://queering.earth/changelog).

## The type plays, and the words do not move

Letters on this site lean, deviate, and refuse to sit on the line. That is not decoration. A page about normativity that sets itself in a tidy grid is arguing against itself.

It also is not a trade. Every page here has a **plain view** — the control at the top of this one. It turns off the play, the motion, and the display faces, and leaves the words exactly as they are. One page, two ways to read it, and no second-class copy. Nothing on this site will ever be readable only in the decorated version.

Say *lilac* and something answers. For Helen it is always Eliot, instantly — the four lines that open *The Waste Land*, which file the flower on the cruel side of the season rather than the kind one.

Specimen — the lilacs

> April is the cruellest month, breeding Lilacs out of the dead land, mixing Memory and desire, stirring Dull roots with spring rain.

T. S. Eliot, [*The Waste Land*](https://www.poetryfoundation.org/poems/47311/the-waste-land), lines 1–4 (1922). Public domain in the United States; wording checked against [Project Gutenberg’s transcription](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/1321) of the 1922 text.

*Syringa vulgaris*, the common lilac, drawn from the shrubs in Ryan’s yard. *Lilac Wine* is James Shelton’s, written in 1950 for the revue *Dance Me a Song*; the readings that stayed with us are [Nina Simone’s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wild_Is_the_Wind_(Nina_Simone_album)) on *Wild Is the Wind* (1966) and [Jeff Buckley’s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grace_(Jeff_Buckley_album)) on *Grace* (1994). One song, read three ways — which is this site’s method, sung.
